Where Can a Robot End Up?

A visual guide to figuring out which squares a sliding robot can reach.

The game

Picture a grid with a few robots on it. When you pick a robot and push it, the robot doesn't stop after one square — it slides in the direction you pushed until it crashes into another robot. It stops in the square right next to whatever blocked it.

Two rules:

  1. No stopping on purpose. Once a robot starts sliding, it can't stop until something blocks it.
  2. Another robot has to block it. If you push a robot toward an empty part of the board, and nothing is in the way, the robot would fly off the edge. That's not allowed. You can only make a move if there's a blocker to catch it.

Here's the question this page is about:

If you play for a long time, making lots of different moves, which squares could a robot possibly visit? And which ones are just impossible to ever reach?

We'll start with loose guesses ("robots can't leave this big region") and tighten them, step by step, until we get the exact answer. We'll use square boards (robots slide up/down/left/right) for most of the story, and talk about hex boards — which have two extra diagonal directions — at the end.

Throughout the page we'll keep coming back to the same four robots, arranged in a diamond, so you can watch the picture get tighter and tighter.

1 The box

The simplest idea: draw a box around the robots. Use the leftmost robot's column as the left wall, the rightmost as the right wall, the topmost as the ceiling, and the bottommost as the floor. That gives you a rectangle.

Claim: No robot can ever slide outside this box, no matter how many moves you make.

Why this is true

Think about the right wall of the box. For a robot to land to the right of that wall, it would have to slide right and crash into a blocker that's even further right. But nobody is further right — every robot is on or inside that wall, so there's nothing out there to catch it. The move is illegal.

The same logic works for the left wall, the ceiling, and the floor. Once all the robots are inside the box, they have to stay inside the box, because there's no blocker outside it to stop a slide.

This is the loosest guess we'll make. The box includes lots of squares that the robots probably can't actually reach — we're just saying they definitely can't reach anything outside it.

2 The rubber band

The box wastes space. Look at our diamond again: those four robots form a clear diamond shape, but the box treats them like they fill a big rectangle. The corners of the box are obviously wasted.

Here's a tighter idea. Imagine the four robots are pegs stuck into a board. Now snap a rubber band around all of them. The rubber band pulls tight, making a shape. That shape is called the "rubber-band shape" (math people call it the convex hull, but the rubber band is the easier picture).

Why robots can't escape the rubber band

The rubber band works the same way the box did, just in more directions. Pick any direction you like — not just up-down or left-right, but any angle. Look at the "edge" of the rubber band in that direction.

For a robot to slide past that edge, it would need a blocker even further past the edge. But every robot is on or inside the rubber band, so no blocker is out there. The slide fails.

Since this works for every direction at once, the rubber band traps everyone — in the same way the box did, but with the corners pulled in.

Tighter and tighter

You don't have to jump straight from "box" to "rubber band." There are in-between versions, where you cut off the corners of the box one set at a time. Here's the same idea applied to a different group of robots, going from box → octagon → full rubber-band shape:

For a handful of robots, the rubber-band shape is easy to draw by eye. It's just the polygon you'd get by wrapping string around the outermost robots.

3 Fill the gaps

The rubber band is tighter than the box, but it still wastes a lot of space. Look at the diamond: the rubber band is a filled 13-square diamond — but the robots are only really at the four tips. The middle of the diamond isn't obviously reachable.

Here's an idea that uses a different kind of reasoning. Instead of thinking about directions, think about rows and columns.

Ask yourself: when a robot slides, where does it end up? The answer is always: on the same row as its blocker (if it slid left or right), or on the same column as its blocker (if it slid up or down). And the landing square is always between the robot's starting spot and the blocker.

So here's a recipe for a tighter shape:

  1. Start with just the squares where the robots sit — those are your starting "filled" squares.
  2. Row pass. Look at each row. If it has two or more filled squares, fill every square between the leftmost one and the rightmost one.
  3. Column pass. Do the same for every column — find the topmost and bottommost filled squares, and fill everything in between.
  4. Did anything change? If the row or column pass added any new squares, go back to step 2 and do it again. If nothing changed, you're done.

The "do it again" part is important. Sometimes filling rows creates new alignments for columns, and filling columns creates new alignments for rows. We'll see an example shortly.

For now, let's apply the recipe to our diamond. Since each pair of robots that share a row or column is already at the tips of the diamond, one round of row-filling plus column-filling does the whole job, giving a plus-sign shape:

Why robots can't escape the plus-shape

Remember: when a robot slides, it lands between itself and the blocker, on the row or column they share. If the robot started inside the plus-shape, and the blocker is also inside the plus-shape, then every square between them — on the same row or column — is also inside the plus-shape. (That's exactly how we built it: we filled in all the gaps along rows and columns.)

So the landing square has to be inside the plus-shape too. Once everyone starts inside, nobody can leave.

When one pass isn't enough

The diamond was a tidy case: one round of row-filling plus column-filling gave the answer, and a second round added nothing. But sometimes the algorithm has to loop several times before it stabilizes. Here's an example.

Five robots on a 5×5 grid: at the top-left corner, the top-right corner, the bottom-left corner, and two "staircase" robots in the middle. Watch what happens as we run the algorithm, one pass at a time. Green squares are the new squares added in that pass:

Walking through it:

  • Start. Just the 5 robot squares.
  • Pass 1 — fill rows. The only row with two robots is row 0 (robots at columns 0 and 4). Fill the 3 squares between them.
  • Pass 1 — fill columns. Now scan columns. Col 0 has robots at the top and bottom — fill the 3 squares between. Col 2 has a robot at row 2 and a newly-filled square from the row pass at (0, 2) — fill (1, 2) between them. Col 3 has a robot at row 3 and a new square at (0, 3) — fill (1, 3) and (2, 3). Six new squares total.
  • Pass 2 — fill rows. Did anything change in pass 1? Yes — we added 9 squares. So we run the row pass again. Row 1 now has squares at columns 0, 2, and 3 (all added in pass 1) — fill the gap at column 1. Row 2 same: fill (2, 1). Row 3 has squares at columns 0 and 3 — fill the two in between. Four new squares.
  • Pass 2 — fill columns. Scan every column. Columns 1, 2, and 3 now have no gaps (they run contiguously from row 0 to row 3). Nothing to add. The shape didn't change on this pass — stop!

Notice the cascading effect: the row pass adds squares that create new column alignments, which add squares that create new row alignments, and so on. Each pass sets up the next. The process stops as soon as one pass adds zero new squares.

Why the algorithm always stops

Each pass either adds at least one new square, or adds nothing and stops. The shape only grows — we never remove a square. And the board has only so many squares (25 on 5×5, 49 on 7×7). So we can't keep growing forever. Eventually a pass has to add zero squares, and we stop.

In practice it's really fast: even for big puzzles with lots of robots, the algorithm almost always stabilizes within 2 or 3 full rounds.

This shape is called the orthogonal convex hull if you want the fancy name. We'll just call it the plus-shape — even when, like the 5-robot example above, it's actually a more complicated staircase. The rule is always the same: no gaps across any row, no gaps down any column.

The plus-shape still isn't the real answer

The plus-shape is way better than the box or the rubber band, but it still says "yes, this square is reachable" for some squares that are actually impossible. Here's a simple example:

Two robots, at columns 0 and 5, sitting in the same row. The plus-shape is the whole row between them — 6 squares. But think about what moves are actually possible:

  • A slides right. B stops it. A lands at column 4 (one square left of B).
  • B slides left. A stops it. B lands at column 1 (one square right of A).

Now A and B are sitting right next to each other — adjacent. Whichever one you try to slide, the other is right there, and you only move one square at best. You can never get into the middle.

The real reachable set is just 4 squares: {0, 1, 4, 5}. The plus-shape said 6. Our rule missed something: it only looked at which squares line up, not at whether the robots can physically get in each other's way.

To find the exact answer, we need a different approach.

4 Try every move

The only way to get the exact answer is to check every possible sequence of moves. That's what a computer can do for us:

  1. Start with the robots in their original positions.
  2. For every legal move, write down what the board looks like after that move.
  3. For every new board, repeat step 2.
  4. Keep going until no new boards show up.
  5. Collect all the squares that a robot ever landed on across all those boards. That's your answer.

This is called a breadth-first search (BFS). It explores outward from the starting position one move at a time, like ripples spreading on a pond.

The catch: the number of boards to check can get huge. With just 4 or 5 robots on a 7×7 board, you might see thousands or tens of thousands of different arrangements before the search finishes. But for small puzzles it's very doable — and it gives you the exact reachable set, with no overestimate.

All four ideas, from loose to exact

Every idea is tighter than the one before. The box contains the rubber-band shape, which contains the plus-shape, which contains the true reachable set:

What about hex boards?

So far we've only talked about square boards, where robots slide up, down, left, and right. The game also has hex boards, where robots can slide in two extra diagonal directions. That's 6 directions total:

Here's the good news: the box and the rubber band work exactly the same way on hex boards. The same "no blocker out there means no slide out there" argument works for any direction, diagonals included.

But the plus-shape idea needs a small fix. Why? Remember how we built it: we filled in rows and columns, because a sliding robot always lands on the same row or column as its blocker. On a hex board that's still true for the 4 normal directions — but for the 2 diagonal ones, the robot and blocker line up along a diagonal, not a row or column. The plus-shape misses that.

The fix: also fill in gaps along diagonals (the diagonal that goes up-right and down-left). Now the shape has three "arms" instead of two — it looks like a three-armed star:

Why this matters in the game

These aren't just math ideas — the game uses them. In the Settings panel, you can pick which shape to use for shading "unreachable" squares. Picking a tighter shape darkens more squares, making it easier to focus on the ones that actually matter.

And here's a really useful trick: if the exit cell (the center) ever falls outside one of these shapes, the puzzle is unsolvable from that position. The exit-robot has to reach the center, and if the center isn't even inside the shape that contains the robot, it can't happen.

So if you've moved your robots into a weird spot and the plus-shape suddenly doesn't include the center anymore, you know you've made a mistake. Undo and try again.
